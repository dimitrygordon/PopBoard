## Slide 1: DIY Project Based Learning for Math and Science

**Words:** 87  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:35

Hi everyone, I'm Dimitry. My Book Share is DIY Project Based Learning for Math and Science by Heather Wolpert-Gawron, an award-winning middle school teacher and Edutopia blogger. I'm a physics and Earth and space science teacher in training, so I picked this book because I want my students doing science, not just reading about it. Over the next fifteen minutes or so, I'll teach you four big ideas, the key terms behind them, what each idea looks like in real classrooms, and three questions to chew on.

---

## Slide 2: Engage: Is your unit a line or a story?

**Words:** 124  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:50

Wolpert-Gawron opens with a comparison I can't get out of my head. At Disneyland, the story of a ride starts the moment you step into line. The walls, the ceiling, even the ground you walk on all build toward the ride. At a lot of other parks, you just wait in a metal maze and then you ride. She says most of our teaching is the metal maze: disconnected daily lessons that each teach a skill but never add up to a story. Project Based Learning, or PBL, is the Disneyland version. Every lesson lives inside one story about a problem that has to be solved. So here's a question to hold onto: is your current unit a line, or is it a story?

---

## Slide 3: Idea 1: PBL is a unit, not a project

**Words:** 111  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:44

Idea one: PBL is a unit, not a project. The author gives us an elevator speech for it. PBL is learning through identifying a real world problem and developing its solution, and kids show what they learn as they journey through the unit, not just at the end. That last part is the key. She draws a sharp line between assigning a project and doing Project Based Learning. Assigning a project is just an assessment you tack on at the end. PBL is the whole unit: every lesson, exploration, piece of research, and presentation, all driven by one driving question. In her words, it's about the process, not the end project.

---

## Slide 4: Idea 1: How the story gets built

**Words:** 140  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:56

So how does she build that story? Every unit in the book uses the same moves. It starts with an entry event, something that hooks students into the problem. In the Galapagos unit, an eighth grader films a video playing the head of the Smithsonian, inviting seventh grade "explorers" to research an island and decide which species to save. Then comes role play. Students aren't students anymore; they're doctors, inventors, field scientists, or theme park engineers. And the work goes to an authentic audience, meaning real people outside the teacher who care about the answer: a board, a principal, local STEM professionals, or other classes. She also leans on the author A. J. Juliani's distinction between algorithmic tasks, which follow one set path to one answer, and heuristic tasks, which require trial and error. PBL lives in the heuristic world.

## Slide 5: Idea 1: Put Into Practice

**Words:** 148  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:59

Here's what idea one looks like across classrooms. In an elementary roller coaster unit the author borrows from teachers Emily Cruz and Melanie Valencia, kids build coasters out of foam tubing, marbles, and tape, tackling a hill, then a curve, then a loop, and recording why each trial worked or failed. In a middle school Invention Unit, kids find an everyday problem at home and pitch a solution. In my own high school physics class, I'd turn that coaster idea into an energy unit. The driving question might be: can your coaster carry a marble through a loop without falling off at the top? Students predict, build, film their trials in slow motion on a phone, and revise. The final pitch goes to a "park safety board" made up of other teachers. Same skills as a worksheet on energy, but now the worksheet has a reason to exist.

## Slide 6: Idea 2: Design toward what you love

**Words:** 134  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:54

Idea two: design toward what you love, then backplan to the standards. This one surprised me. The author admits she doesn't start with the standards. She calls standards-first planning, in her words, "a stinky model." Instead, she uses three steps. One: design toward what you love, meaning your interests and the interests of the age group you teach. Two: look back at the standards. Three: fill in the gaps. Her reasoning is that enthusiasm is "trickle down." If the teacher is bored, the kids will be too. She calls this a teacher's maker movement: just like students make products, teachers should get to make curriculum. And when one teacher can't hit every standard, she says to find a cross-curricular partner. The Galapagos unit itself came from her collaborating with a seventh grade science teacher.

---

## Slide 7: Idea 2: The checklist is a contract

**Words:** 124  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:50

The tool that keeps this from turning into chaos is the checklist. Every unit starts with a pacing checklist that lays out the assignments and deadlines, and it goes to students, parents, and administrators on day one. She backplans it by entering the final presentation date first and working backward. She also leaves a few empty rows on purpose, because a good guide has to stay flexible when the learning reveals a gap. She treats the checklist like a contract: the teacher promises not to wing it, and the students promise to plan ahead. One line from the book stuck with me: "The mystery defeats achievement." If we want students to own their learning, we have to show them where the unit is going.

## Slide 8: Idea 2: A tension worth naming

**Words:** 133  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:53

Now, let me push on this idea a little. In our coursework we've been studying backward design, which starts with the learning goals and the evidence of learning before any activities. At first, Wolpert-Gawron's approach sounds like the opposite. But look closer. Her steps two and three are backward design; she just puts the hook first. I think the honest takeaway is a tension worth naming. Starting from passion can make a unit come alive, but if you never get to step three, you end up with a fun project that misses the standards. For my Earth and space class, I'm drafting an air quality unit. I'd start with what hooks my students, the air in their own town, and then audit it against the standards and fill the holes before day one.

## Slide 9: Idea 3: Communication is the content

**Words:** 147  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:59

Idea three: communication is the content. This idea runs through every chapter. The author quotes her earlier book: "If it doesn't exist outside of school, then it isn't worthy enough to be taught within school." So her students don't write generic essays. They write the genres real STEM professionals write. In the Genius Unit, students play doctors writing a scientific lab report to a patient's family. In the Invention Unit, they write a problem statement, which is a short, researched case that a problem is real, plus three to five questions to guide more research. They also write a business letter asking a company to fund their invention. In the Galapagos and Theme Park units, they write an executive summary, a short pitch to a busy decision maker. Her point is that writing isn't a break from math and science. It's how math and science get shared.

## Slide 10: Idea 3: Writing with numbers

**Words:** 150  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 1:00

Two tools from this idea are worth stealing. The first is writing with numeracy, which means teaching students how to put numbers into their writing correctly, as evidence. She builds a reference sheet of rules, like spelling out a number that starts a sentence. The second is BIRAC, an engineering-style outline she uses for the Theme Park executive summary. B is background, posing the question. I is issues, what might get in the way. R is recommendations. A is analysis, the evidence that the plan works. And C is consequences, what happens if the board says yes or no. The Invention Unit also has real math in it: a break-even analysis, where cost equals variable cost times units plus fixed cost, written C equals v x plus F, and revenue equals price times units, written R equals p x. Students graph both lines in Desmos and find where they cross.

## Slide 11: Idea 3: Assess writing quickly

**Words:** 141  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:56

Of course, if every STEM teacher assigns writing, someone has to grade it. So each unit ends with a tip on how to assess writing quickly. Tip one: focus feedback on one skill at a time, instead of what she calls a "bloody explosion of red pen." Tip two: use an oral feedback sheet. You conference with the student, and the student takes the notes, because, as she asks, isn't the one doing the work the one doing the learning? Tip three: use a key of feedback symbols instead of writing full sentences. Tip four: comment rather than correct. Instead of fixing errors, tell the student, "I see three equations in your lab that don't add up," and let them hunt. Tip five: train students to give the first round of feedback to each other before the work ever reaches you.

## Slide 12: Idea 3: Put Into Practice

**Words:** 133  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:53

Here's idea three in practice. In a middle school math class, the break-even analysis gives students a real reason to write and graph linear equations. In a biology class, the Galapagos executive summary turns natural selection and biodiversity into a funding argument. In my Earth and space air quality unit, students could pull real readings from the EPA's AirNow site, write them up using the numeracy rules, and present a BIRAC summary to a local audience, maybe the school board, on how to protect students on poor air days. And in my physics class, a lab report written as an engineer's memo to a client is still a lab report. It just has an audience now. That's the shift: the same content, but students have to explain it to someone who needs it.

## Slide 13: Idea 4: Teach them to be teachers

**Words:** 147  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:59

Idea four: teach them to be teachers. The final unit in the book is called Teach Them to Be Teachers, and it's built on a line she credits to Aristotle: teaching is the highest form of understanding. Students pick a topic they know and love, teach it to the class with a lesson plan and an activity, and then write a persuasive letter to an administrator pitching it as a new elective. But the deeper move happens all through the book: students learn to write assessments, not just take them. She teaches them Costa's Levels of Questioning. Level one is input: recall, define, list. Level two is process: compare, infer, sequence. Level three is output: predict, hypothesize, evaluate, create. She makes it physical with a two-pound weight, a twenty-five-pound weight, and a hundred-pound barbell she can't actually lift. Each level is a harder workout for the brain.

## Slide 14: Idea 4: Students write the tests

**Words:** 139  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:56

Three tools make this idea real. First, the student-created assessment. In the Genius Unit, each student reads one article and builds a ten-question quiz in Google Forms for their classmates, mixing question levels and formats. Second, the student-created rubric. Students translate the district rubric into their own words, and the author insists on one thing: the top score is called "Able to Teach This Topic." Third, the backchannel. While a classmate presents, the audience types questions into a shared Google Doc in real time. And here's the twist: the author grades the audience on their questions, counting how many are level two or level three. She also has student teams write a collaboration constitution, a group contract, at the start of a unit, and at the end they can only grade each other on rules they wrote into it.

## Slide 15: Idea 4: Put Into Practice

**Words:** 121  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:48

Here's how idea four travels. In an ELA or history class, students can teach a short lesson on a skill and score their own classmates' quizzes, which is real data analysis. In a math class, the "Able to Teach" rubric level could mean a student explains a solution well enough that a peer can solve the next problem. In my physics class, I'd have each lab group teach one concept, say friction, momentum, or energy transfer, to the rest of the class, and write a three-question quiz with one question at each Costa level. I'd also borrow the backchannel. My quieter students often have the best questions, and a shared doc gives them a voice without having to raise a hand.

## Slide 16: A critical lens

**Words:** 131  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:52

Now, an honest critique. This book came out in 2016, and some of its tools are gone. TodaysMeet, the backchannel site she uses, shut down years ago. So treat the tools as examples and swap in what your school uses, like a shared Google Doc or Canvas discussion. Second, the Teach Them to Be Teachers unit uses a multiple intelligences quiz and mentions learning styles. Research by Pashler and colleagues found little evidence that matching instruction to learning styles improves learning, so I'd keep the activity variety and drop the labels. Third, look at the Genius Unit's student lab reports. They're creative and well written, but some of their brain science claims are shaky. Authentic work still needs a content check. In PBL, the teacher's job shifts, but it doesn't disappear.

## Slide 17: Key terms at a glance

**Words:** 107  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:43

Here are the book's key terms in one place. A driving question is the real-world problem that anchors the whole unit. An entry event hooks students into it. An authentic audience is someone outside the teacher who needs the answer. Backplanning means setting the final date first and working backward on the pacing checklist. A problem statement is a short, researched case that a problem is real. BIRAC is the five-part outline for an executive summary. Costa's Levels of Questioning sort questions into input, process, and output. And 'Able to Teach This Topic' is the top rubric score: a student understands it well enough to teach it.

## Slide 18: Conclude with direction

**Words:** 92  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:37

So here's what I hope you take away. One: PBL is a unit, not a project, so build the whole story around a real problem. Two: design toward what you and your students love, then backplan to the standards and fill the gaps, with a checklist that makes the journey visible. Three: in STEM, communication is the content, so give students real genres and real audiences, and use quick feedback tools so you're not buried. Four: teach students to be teachers, by having them write the questions, the rubrics, and the lessons.

## Slide 19: Discussion questions

**Words:** 53  |  **Est. @ 150 wpm:** 0:21

Here are my three discussion questions. Each one asks you to take an idea from this video into your own classroom. First, planning order and the empty rows of the checklist. Second, a real-world genre paired with a feedback tip. Third, other ways students can prove they're able to teach. Thanks for watching.

**Discussion questions (full text):**
1. In the video, Wolpert-Gawron's planning order (design toward what you love, then look back at the standards, then fill the gaps) was compared with backward design. Choose a unit you will teach. Which order would you plan it in, and what would you put in the empty rows of your pacing checklist to keep it flexible? Explain how your choice connects to an idea from the presentation.
2. The presentation shared five ways to assess writing quickly and argued that in STEM, communication is the content. Pick one real-world genre from the video (problem statement, executive summary with BIRAC, business letter, or lab report) and describe how you would use it in your subject. Which feedback tip would you pair it with, and how does that tip shift who does the work?
3. The book insists that the top level of a rubric be called "Able to Teach This Topic." In addition to the approaches in the presentation (student-created quizzes, student-translated rubrics, backchanneling), what other way could students in your classroom prove they can teach a concept? What evidence would convince you, and what is one risk of the approach?
