Check: an open–closed tube with L = 0.85 m at 20 °C has f₁ ≈ 343/(4×0.85) ≈ 100.9 Hz — and it has no 2nd harmonic; the next mode is the 3rd harmonic at ≈ 302.6 Hz. Idealised: the open-end antinode is drawn exactly at the rim; real tubes need an end correction (≈ 0.3× diameter).

The Big Idea

Standing waves are interference with rules. A wave reflecting off a fixed end (or the pressure/displacement conditions at a tube's ends) interferes with itself. For most frequencies the interference is messy and weak, but at the natural frequencies — set by length and wave speed through fₙ = (n/2L)·√(T/μ) on a string, nv/2L in an open tube, or odd multiples of v/4L in a closed tube — reflections reinforce, nodes and antinodes lock into place, and a small driver builds a large response: resonance. Change velocity or length and every harmonic moves together; change only the harmonic mix and pitch stays while timbre changes. The same “allowed modes only” logic describes guitar strings, organ pipes, and quantised modes in other physical systems.
