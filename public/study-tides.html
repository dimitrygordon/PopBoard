Real coasts vary. The equilibrium model gives two equal highs a day. Real oceans slosh in basins: many Pacific coasts are mixed (two unequal highs), much of the Gulf Coast is mostly diurnal (one high a day — modelled here as a schematic diurnal harmonic, as basin resonance favours it), and the Bay of Fundy resonates to ~16 m ranges. Continents, basin shape, depth, rotation and resonance set your local tide table. Never use this sandbox as a forecast.

The Sun’s total pull on Earth is ~180× the Moon’s — it keeps us in orbit! But its pull is almost the same on near and far sides, so the difference — the tide-raising part — is small. The Moon is ~400× closer; its pull changes noticeably across one Earth diameter.

Equilibrium Model vs the Real Ocean

What this sandbox is: Newton’s equilibrium tide — the smooth shape a planet-wide ocean would settle into under the Moon’s and Sun’s gravity gradients. It explains the why: two bulges, ~12 h 25 min spacing, spring–neap rhythm, ~50 min daily drift.

What it is not: a tide table. Real oceans are blocked by continents, slosh in basins, are steered by Earth’s rotation, and can resonate — the Bay of Fundy reaches ~16 m, the largest range on Earth, while some places (Gulf Coast, parts of the Pacific) get one main high a day, and amphidromic points get almost none. Local predictions need harmonic analysis of real measurements.
